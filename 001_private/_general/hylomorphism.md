**Hylomorphism** is Aristotle’s account of material things as compounds of **matter** (*hyle*) and **form** (*morphe* or *eidos*). Matter is what something is made from; form explains what makes it the kind of thing it is, including its characteristic capacities. These are principles of one thing, not two independently existing objects joined together.

## Matter, form, and actuality

- **Matter** is potentially a particular thing: bronze can become a statue. This does not mean ordinary matter is wholly indeterminate — bronze already has properties that enable and constrain what it can become.
- **Form** actualises that potential: a particular configuration makes the bronze a statue. In living beings, form is more than outward shape; it accounts for the organisation and capacities of a living whole.
- **Formal and final explanation** are closely connected in Aristotle’s biology: understanding an organ involves understanding what it does and what it is for. This links hylomorphism to [teleology](teleology.md), not merely to geometry or information processing.

## Soul as the form of a living body

In *De Anima* II.1, Aristotle calls the soul the **first actuality of a natural body potentially having life**. Here, “soul” (*psyche*) means the principle of life, not specifically consciousness: plants have nutritive capacities, animals also perceive, and humans also possess intellect.

“First actuality” distinguishes possessing a capacity from exercising it. A sleeping animal remains alive and capable of perception even when it is not actively perceiving. The soul is therefore not an extra agent operating the body, nor simply a record of its current activity.

This broadly resists a picture of soul and body as two separate substances. Aristotle’s treatment of intellect (*nous*) raises disputed questions about separability, however, so the account should not be reduced to an unqualified modern materialism.

## Change and identity

Hylomorphism distinguishes changes **in a persisting substance** from changes **in which a substance comes into or goes out of existence**:

- **Accidental change:** an animal grows or changes colour while remaining the same animal.
- **Substantial change:** an animal dies; material constituents remain, but the living organism does not persist merely because some of its matter does.

The statue analogy has limits. Bronze can survive losing its statue-shape, whereas Aristotle calls a dead eye an eye only “homonymously”: it retains the name or appearance, but not the capacity for sight. An organised living body is not neutral material waiting for a soul to be installed. How to reconcile this with matter’s role as the persisting subject of change is a genuine interpretive problem, not something the analogy resolves.

## Comparison with functionalism

There is a useful affinity with [functionalism](functionalism.md): both give explanatory importance to capacities and organisation rather than merely listing constituent materials. But **Aristotelian form is not simply software**, and resemblance is not proof of a direct historical ancestry.

[Multiple realisability](../books/how_to_build_a_brain/multiple_realisability.md) is a further claim about which different materials can realise the same mental properties. Hylomorphism does not by itself establish broad substrate independence: matter must be suitable for the relevant capacities. How far Aristotle permits alternative realisers, and whether he should count as a functionalist, remain interpretive questions.

## Implications for brain emulation

For [invariant brain emulation](../../002_profession/eightsix/invariant_brain_emulation.md), conserving “form” across a substrate change is a **modern analogy**, not a literal consequence of Aristotle’s theory. It prompts three distinct questions:

1. Does the replacement preserve the relevant capacities, rather than merely outward behaviour?
2. Does it sustain one continuing living individual, or produce a new individual with similar capacities?
3. What warrants extending either conclusion to conscious experience and personal identity?

Likewise, [emergent properties](../videos/emergent_properties.md) offer a comparison about organisation and explanatory levels, not an automatic identification of form with a new force or a temporally emerging property.

> [!summary] Key distinction
> Preserving material constituents, preserving a kind of organisation, and preserving the same individual are different claims. Hylomorphism helps articulate the distinction; it does not settle substrate transfer.

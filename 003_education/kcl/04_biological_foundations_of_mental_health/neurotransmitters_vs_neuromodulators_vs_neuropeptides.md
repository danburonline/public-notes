#core/appliedneuroscience

## Signalling roles and molecular form

“Neurotransmitter” and “neuromodulator” primarily describe signalling roles and effects, whereas “neuropeptide” describes a molecular form. They are overlapping rather than rigidly nested categories: the same molecule can have different effects depending on its receptors, location, release pattern, and circuit.

## Neurotransmitters

Neurotransmitters are endogenous **chemical messengers released by neurons to affect a target neuron, muscle cell, or gland cell.** They can act across a chemical synapse, including a neuromuscular junction. Their effects may be fast, through ligand-gated ion channels, or slower, through metabotropic receptors and intracellular signalling.

### Key Features

- They are synthesised by neurons or their terminals.
- Small-molecule transmitters are commonly stored in small clear synaptic vesicles; storage and release depend on the molecule.
- They can be released from presynaptic terminals in response to an action potential; some signalling is extrasynaptic.
- Can directly change the target cell’s conductance or alter its state through receptor-dependent signalling.
- Examples of signalling molecules include glutamate, GABA, dopamine, serotonin, acetylcholine, and norepinephrine; their effects are receptor- and circuit-dependent.

## Neuromodulators

Neuromodulation is a **signalling role in which a chemical changes the responsiveness, excitability, or synaptic transmission of target cells.** A neuromodulator can also be a neurotransmitter in a different context; modulation does not require a molecule to lack direct effects.

### Key Features

- They can influence several cells or synapses, not only the immediately neighbouring target.
- Effects are often slower or longer-lasting than fast ionotropic transmission, but timescale depends on the receptor, location, and circuit.
- They can amplify, diminish, or otherwise reshape responses to other signals.
- Clearance is molecule- and context-specific and can involve diffusion, transporters, and enzymatic degradation.

## Neuropeptides

Neuropeptides are **peptide molecules used by neurons for signalling.** They are a molecular category, not simply a functional subtype of neuromodulator: a neuropeptide can contribute to neurotransmission or neuromodulation depending on its receptors and site of action.

### Key Features

- They are synthesised as precursor proteins, processed into peptides, and commonly transported to terminals in larger dense-core vesicles.
- Release often accompanies release of a small-molecule transmitter, but co-release and release conditions depend on the cell and circuit.
- They can act synaptically or extrasynaptically. Their spatial spread is constrained by extracellular geometry, binding, uptake, and degradation rather than being unlimited or uniformly diffuse.
- Their effects are often slower or more prolonged, but their time course and range depend on the peptide, receptors, and location.
- Examples include endorphins, vasopressin, and oxytocin.

## Differences

1. **Category:** Neurotransmitter and neuromodulator describe what a signal does in a circuit; neuropeptide describes what kind of molecule it is. These descriptions can overlap.
2. **Speed and duration:** Small-molecule signalling can be fast through ionotropic receptors or slower through metabotropic receptors. Peptide signalling is often slower or prolonged, but no single timescale applies to every molecule.
3. **Location and clearance:** Signals may act synaptically or extrasynaptically. Diffusion, transporters, receptor binding, and enzymatic degradation all contribute to clearance in molecule- and context-specific combinations.
